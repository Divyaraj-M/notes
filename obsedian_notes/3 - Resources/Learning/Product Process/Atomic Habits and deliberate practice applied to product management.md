# How great PMs build product instinct

**Product sense is not a gift — it is pattern recognition forged through deliberate, repeatable habits.** This is the single most consistent finding across the work of Teresa Torres, Marty Cagan, Shreyas Doshi, Lenny Rachitsky, Gibson Biddle, Julie Zhuo, and Jackie Bavaro. Every major PM thought leader explicitly rejects the notion that product judgment is innate. Instead, they converge on a model where structured routines — daily observation, weekly customer exposure, monthly reflection, and continuous decision-tracking — compound over **2–5 years** into what outsiders perceive as intuition. The practical implication: a junior PM who adopts proven cadences and mental models will develop product sense significantly faster than one who simply accumulates calendar time in the role.

What follows is a synthesis of the most credible, widely-cited frameworks for building that instinct — organized from foundational mental models through specific daily, weekly, and monthly systems.

---

## The three frameworks that define modern product discovery

Three bodies of work form the intellectual backbone of how top PMs think and work: Teresa Torres's _Continuous Discovery Habits_, Marty Cagan's _Inspired/Empowered_ model, and Shreyas Doshi's mental model toolkit. Each attacks a different dimension of the problem.

**Teresa Torres** defines continuous discovery as "at a minimum, weekly touchpoints with customers by the team building the product, where they conduct small research activities in pursuit of a desired outcome." Her system rests on six interlocking habits: outcome-oriented thinking, continuous interviewing (minimum one customer per week), mapping opportunities via the **Opportunity Solution Tree** (OST), prioritizing opportunities over solutions, generating at least three solutions per opportunity, and rapid assumption testing. The OST is her signature artifact — a living visual tree with the desired outcome at top, customer opportunities in the middle, candidate solutions below, and assumption tests at the bottom. It externalizes the PM's thinking and forces exploration of the problem space before jumping to features. Torres insists: "Most teams jump straight from an outcome to solutions. The opportunity space is where the magic happens." The cadence is weekly: interview, update the tree, run at least one assumption test.

**Marty Cagan** draws a hard boundary between discovery (figuring out what to build) and delivery (building it). Discovery must address four risks simultaneously — **value** (will customers use it?), **usability** (can they figure it out?), **feasibility** (can we build it?), and **business viability** (does it work for the business?). Cagan argues that product sense emerges from deep immersion across four knowledge domains: the customer, the data, the industry, and the business. He recommends PMs spend hours per week in direct customer exposure, run prototype tests weekly, and maintain a compelling product vision. His core claim: "Behind every great product, there is someone who led the product team to combine technology and design to solve real problems in a way that meets the needs of the business. That requires product sense, and product sense comes from immersion."

**Shreyas Doshi** (former PM leader at Stripe, Twitter, and Google) contributes a toolkit of mental models that operate at a different altitude. His **LNO Framework** classifies every task as Leverage (quality of effort creates 10 x–100 x impact), Neutral (adequate execution suffices), or Overhead (just get it done). Most PMs burn out doing everything at an A+ level; the LNO framework redirects energy toward the work that actually compounds. His **pre-mortem** practice — imagining a project has already failed and asking "what went wrong?" — surfaces hidden risks with psychological safety. His **thinking-in-bets** model frames product decisions as wagers with expected value rather than binary right/wrong calls, paired with a **decision journal** to track predictions against outcomes. Doshi is emphatic: "The most underrated PM skill is not prioritization, not strategy, not data analysis. It's taste."

## Atomic Habits and deliberate practice applied to product management

General habit-building science maps onto PM skill development with surprising precision. James Clear's **identity-based habits** concept is particularly powerful: rather than setting a goal ("ship better features"), adopt an identity ("I am a customer-obsessed PM") and prove it with small daily wins — reading every NPS comment, joining one support call per week, writing customer quotes into every spec. The identity shift drives habit selection organically.

Clear's **habit stacking** formula ("After [current habit], I will [new habit]") creates low-friction routines: "After my morning standup, I spend 5 minutes writing in my decision journal." "After every sprint review, I write 3 bullets on what surprised me." "After reading the weekly metrics email, I write one hypothesis about what's driving the numbers." These micro-habits eliminate the willpower tax that kills most PM development plans.

The harder challenge is applying Anders Ericsson's **deliberate practice** to a field without standardized scoring or immediate feedback. PM outcomes are delayed by months, confounded by market shifts and team execution, and rarely attributable to any single decision. Practitioners have identified five categories of "reps" that approximate deliberate practice:

- **Product teardowns** (weekly, 30 minutes): Pick any product, analyze the target user, the problem solved, the business model, key design decisions, and what you'd change. Lenny Rachitsky, Jackie Bavaro, and Exponent all recommend this as the closest equivalent to musical scales for PMs.
- **Writing practice**: Write one-pagers, strategy documents, or PRDs for real or hypothetical products. Ken Norton (former Google PM partner) argues writing is the single most important PM skill. The act of articulating reasoning builds judgment faster than thinking alone.
- **Estimation exercises**: Before looking up metrics, estimate them — daily active users for a product, conversion rates for a funnel, market size for a category. Then compare against reality. This calibrates quantitative intuition over hundreds of reps.
- **Pre-mortems and post-mortems**: Before launching, write down what could go wrong. After launching, compare predictions to reality. This creates the tight feedback loop that PM work otherwise lacks.
- **Simulated decisions**: When reading about a company's product challenge, pause and decide what you would do before reading what they actually did. Jackie Bavaro specifically recommends building a "product intuition library" of annotated decisions.

Donald Schön's reflective practitioner model adds a useful distinction: **reflection-in-action** (adjusting your approach mid-meeting when your framing isn't landing) versus **reflection-on-action** (reviewing after a sprint whether the prioritization was right). Both are trainable. Kolb's experiential learning cycle — concrete experience → reflective observation → abstract conceptualization → active experimentation — provides the meta-structure for turning any PM experience into lasting skill growth.

## What the best PMs actually do every day, week, and month

Across interviews and writing from Lenny Rachitsky, Shreyas Doshi, Ken Norton, Teresa Torres, and practitioners at Google, Stripe, and Airbnb, a remarkably consistent set of routines emerges. These are not theoretical — they are practiced at scale inside top product organizations.

**Daily habits (~45 minutes total)** center on three activities. First, **product dogfooding**: 15 minutes using your own product as a real user every morning, noting friction, confusion, and delight. Marty Cagan considers this non-negotiable. At Google, this practice evolved into the **friction log** — a timestamped, emotion-annotated record of using a product end-to-end (e.g., "9:03 — Filled form. 12 fields. Why so many? Frustrated. Severity: High"). Second, a **5-minute metrics check** against a saved dashboard of 3–5 key health indicators, flagging anomalies immediately. Third, a **daily log** at end of day: key decisions made, important conversations, open questions, what you learned, and tomorrow's number-one priority. Ken Norton and Shreyas Doshi both advocate versions of this practice. Many PMs add 10 minutes of **customer signal scanning** — reading support tickets, NPS comments, or app store reviews to stay close to the voice of the customer.

**Weekly routines (~4–5 hours)** are where product sense compounds most visibly. The single most-cited practice across all sources is the **weekly customer conversation** — Teresa Torres's signature habit, endorsed by Cagan, Rachitsky, and Zhuo alike. The format matters: ask about past behavior ("Tell me about the last time you..."), not hypotheticals. Automate recruiting through in-app prompts. Bring the full product trio (PM, designer, engineer). Beyond customer contact, top PMs maintain a **weekly metrics deep-dive** (30–60 minutes analyzing cohorts, funnels, and experiment results), a **weekly prioritization review** (re-stack-ranking the backlog against new information using frameworks like RICE or LNO), a **competitor intelligence scan** (20–30 minutes reviewing competitor changelogs, job postings, and reviews), and a **2–3 hour protected deep work block** for strategic writing. Shreyas Doshi is vocal about protecting this maker time. The week ends with a **personal retrospective** — 15 minutes covering wins, challenges, learnings, and next-week priorities.

**Monthly and quarterly practices** provide the zoom-out that prevents PMs from drowning in execution. Gibson Biddle's **DHM model** (Delight, Hard-to-copy, Margin-enhancing) serves as a monthly strategy review framework: score each major initiative on all three dimensions, and question anything scoring low across the board. Monthly competitive landscape reviews synthesize weekly intelligence into a structured update — new competitor moves, feature gap analysis, positioning checks, and win/loss trends. Teresa Torres and Erika Hall recommend a monthly **user research synthesis** that consolidates all interview data into searchable themes. Quarterly, the cadence expands to **OKR reviews**, **roadmap recalibrations** (outcome-based, not feature-based), and intensive **customer immersion** — what Intuit calls "Follow Me Home" — where PMs shadow 3–5 customers in their natural environments.

## How junior PMs build product sense over 2–5 years

The consensus across Lenny Rachitsky's surveys of elite PMs, Julie Zhuo's writing on building judgment, Jackie Bavaro's systematic skill-building framework, and Reforge's mental-model curriculum is that product sense rests on **three pillars**: customer immersion, product analysis, and decision volume with feedback loops.

**Customer immersion** is the most-cited factor. Every source in this research — without exception — lists regular direct customer exposure as the primary driver of product sense. The mechanism is straightforward: each conversation adds a data point to the PM's internal model of user behavior, and after hundreds of conversations, PMs begin recognizing patterns before they're explicitly articulated. Torres quantifies the minimum at one conversation per week; Lenny reports that the best PMs at Airbnb and Stripe maintain 2–3 per week.

**Product analysis** develops the comparative lens. Julie Zhuo frames this as studying craft the way an art student studies masters — "carefully, analytically, with attention to detail." The recommended practices include weekly product teardowns, maintaining a running product journal of observations, and reverse-engineering successful products to understand the strategy behind visible decisions. Bavaro recommends building a "product intuition library" — a personal collection of annotated product decisions with analysis of why they worked.

**Decision reps with feedback loops** address the biggest obstacle to PM deliberate practice: delayed feedback. The decision journal (popularized by Shane Parrish and adapted for PM by Shreyas Doshi) is the primary tool. Before each significant decision, document context, options, your choice, expected outcome, and confidence level. Set a review date. Come back in 30, 60, or 90 days and evaluate honestly. Julie Zhuo describes the cycle simply: "making decisions → observing outcomes → reflecting on what you learned → updating your mental models." She emphasizes that junior PMs should **seek more at-bats** — volunteer for more decisions, even small ones — because volume accelerates calibration.

Reforge adds an important structural insight: product sense develops faster when PMs **apply multiple frameworks to the same problem** rather than defaulting to a single lens. Analyze a feature using Jobs-to-Be-Done, then the Kano Model, then Opportunity Scoring, then Biddle's DHM. Each framework reveals different aspects, and the friction between them builds nuanced judgment. Casey Winters, a Reforge instructor, captures the meta-principle: "Top PMs think in systems, not features. They work backward from outcomes, not forward from ideas."

The developmental timeline follows a compounding curve. In **years 1–2**, PMs build foundational habits — daily metrics checks, regular customer conversations, writing practice, and learning canonical frameworks. In **years 3–5**, these habits compound into pattern recognition that begins to feel effortless but is actually built on thousands of data points. Beyond **year 5**, the value of maintaining reflective habits increases to prevent complacency — experienced PMs who stop being deliberate about learning plateau or decline.

## The friction log deserves special attention

One practice stands out for its simplicity and outsized impact on developing product instinct: Google's **friction log**. This is a detailed, timestamped record of using a product end-to-end as a real user, documenting every moment of confusion, friction, delight, or surprise — with emotional annotations. It is not a bug report. The format captures the cognitive and emotional experience: what you tried to do, what happened, how you felt, and the severity of each friction point.

The friction log trains three critical PM muscles simultaneously. First, it builds **empathy fluency** — the ability to see products through users' eyes rather than through the lens of internal feature lists. Second, it sharpens **observational precision** — the habit of noticing subtle UX decisions that most people experience unconsciously. Third, it creates a **documentation habit** that makes product insights shareable and actionable. PMs who maintain weekly friction logs on their own product and monthly logs on competitors develop a calibrated sense of quality that becomes increasingly difficult to fool with surface-level polish.

## Conclusion: systems beat goals, and cadence beats intensity

The most striking finding across this research is the convergence. Despite different vocabularies and emphases, Torres, Cagan, Doshi, Rachitsky, Zhuo, Biddle, and Bavaro are all describing the same underlying architecture for building product judgment: **consistent, structured exposure to customers and products, paired with deliberate reflection on decisions and outcomes, maintained at a sustainable weekly cadence**.

The junior PM who interviews one customer weekly, reviews metrics daily, maintains a friction log, writes in a decision journal, and does one product teardown per week will, within two years, develop sharper product instinct than a PM with five years of passive experience. The key insight from Atomic Habits applied here is that **identity precedes behavior**: decide you are the type of PM who never stops learning from customers, then build the smallest viable habits that prove it. Shreyas Doshi's LNO framework ensures these high-leverage habits don't get crowded out by overhead work. Torres's Opportunity Solution Tree gives the weekly customer data a home. Cagan's four-risk model provides the evaluation lens. And the decision journal — perhaps the most underused tool in the entire PM toolkit — closes the feedback loop that makes every other habit compound.

The system is proven. The only variable is whether you start it.
